# Texture Basics

Exploring textures — primarily learning about the most used types (maps) and the ways to load them. The sample texture used here is a popular wood door texture from [3D Textures](https://3dtextures.me/2019/04/16/door-wood-001/).

## Concept

### What Are Textures (and Why Are They Called Maps)?

A **texture** is a 2D image wrapped onto a 3D surface. The term **"map"** comes from the idea that you're *mapping* a 2D image onto a 3D surface — like unfolding a cardboard box and pasting a picture on each face.

Textures are applied via **materials**. In Three.js, you assign a texture to a material's property (e.g. `map`, `normalMap`, `roughnessMap`), and the material wraps it onto the mesh's geometry.

### The Most Used Texture Types

Every texture below (except Alpha) is a separate file. Together, they form a **PBR texture set** — a collection of maps that follow Physically Based Rendering principles to produce realistic results.

#### 1. Color (Albedo) Map

The simplest and most visually obvious texture. It's just a regular color image — a JPG or PNG with no special encoding. It tells the renderer what color each part of the surface should be, without any lighting or shadow baked in.

```ts
const colorMap = textureLoader.load('/textures/wooden_door/Door_Wood_001_basecolor.jpg');

const material = new THREE.MeshBasicMaterial({ map: colorMap });
```

Applied on: **material** (via `map` property).

![[Door_Wood_001_basecolor.jpg]]

#### 2. Alpha Map

A **grayscale** image that controls transparency. In this scheme:

- **White** = fully visible
- **Black** = fully transparent
- **Gray** = semi-transparent

Think of it like a stencil: the shape of the visible part is cut out of the image. Useful for things like chain-link fences, tree foliage, or any surface that shouldn't be a solid rectangle.

Applied on: **material** (via `alphaMap` property). The material also needs `transparent: true` for alpha to take effect.

![[Door_Wood_001_opacity.jpg]]

#### 3. Height (Displacement) Map

Another **grayscale** image, but this one actually **moves the vertices** of the geometry to create real relief — actual bumps, ridges, and dents that cast real shadows.

- **White** pushes vertices outward
- **Black** pushes vertices inward

For this to work, the geometry needs enough **subdivisions** (segments). A basic box with only 1×1 segments per face won't show any displacement — you need a high-density mesh.

Applied on: **material** (via `displacementMap` property) — but the geometry also needs enough vertices.

![[Door_Wood_001_height.png]]

#### 4. Normal Map

A **purple/bluish** image that fakes the appearance of surface detail without adding any geometry. It works by **changing the face normals** (the direction each face points) so the lighting engine thinks the surface has bumps and dents — even though it's perfectly flat.

Why it looks purple: The RGB channels of the image encode the X, Y, Z components of the normal vector. Since normals mostly point "out" (toward the camera), the blue channel is dominant, giving the map its characteristic blue-purple look.

**Key advantage over height maps:** No extra geometry needed. A simple plane with a normal map can look like a detailed brick wall, while a displacement map would require thousands of extra vertices to achieve the same effect — and still wouldn't be as performant.

Applied on: **material** (via `normalMap` property).

![[Door_Wood_001_normal.jpg]]

#### 5. Ambient Occlusion (AO) Map

A **grayscale** image that adds fake shadows in crevices and corners — places where light would have a harder time bouncing into. It's not physically accurate, but it adds contrast and makes details much easier to see.

Think of it as a "where dirt would collect" map — the darker the area, the more shadowed it appears.

Applied on: **material** (via `aoMap` property).

![[Door_Wood_001_ambientOcclusion.jpg]]

#### 6. Metalness Map

A **grayscale** image that tells the renderer which parts of the surface are metallic and which aren't:

- **White** = metallic
- **Black** = non-metallic (dielectric)

Metallic surfaces reflect light differently — they tint reflections with their own color, while non-metallic surfaces have neutral (white) reflections. This map works in duo with Roughness.

Applied on: **material** (via `metalnessMap` property).

![[Door_Wood_001_metallic.jpg]]

#### 7. Roughness Map

Also a **grayscale** image, and the partner to Metalness. It controls how smooth or rough the surface is:

- **White** = rough (light scatters, reflections are blurry)
- **Black** = smooth (light reflects cleanly, like a mirror)

A perfectly smooth surface (`roughness = 0`) would reflect the environment like chrome. A fully rough surface (`roughness = 1`) scatters light evenly in all directions — like matte paper.

Applied on: **material** (via `roughnessMap` property).

![[Door_Wood_001_roughness.jpg]]

### Other Texture Types (Briefly Mentioned)

Beyond the major seven above, there are others you'll encounter:

- **Environment Map** (cube map) — used for reflections, like seeing the sky reflected on a shiny surface
- **Light Map** — pre-baked lighting for static scenes
- **Emissive Map** — makes parts of the surface glow (e.g. an LED screen, neon signs)
- **Specular Map** — controls specular highlights (legacy, mostly replaced by metalness/roughness)
- **Clearcoat Map** — adds a second layer of shininess on top, like car paint

### PBR (Physically Based Rendering)

PBR is a set of rendering techniques that aim to follow real-life light behavior to produce realistic results. It has become the industry standard — game engines (Unreal, Unity), 3D software (Blender, Substance Painter), and web libraries (Three.js) all support it.

Two core PBR properties:

- **Metalness** — Is this surface a metal or a non-metal?
- **Roughness** — How smooth or rough is the surface?

Together, these two (often combined with the other maps) determine how light interacts with the material. The PBR model in Three.js is based on the [Filament](https://google.github.io/filament/Filament.html) rendering engine's principles.

### Loading Textures

There are three ways to load textures, from lowest to highest level:

#### 1. Native JavaScript `Image` Object

Under the hood, all texture loading is this — a browser `Image` fetching a file, then creating a `THREE.Texture` from it:

```ts
const colorImage = new Image();
const colorTexture = new THREE.Texture(colorImage);

colorImage.onload = () => {
  colorTexture.needsUpdate = true; // MUST set this — tells Three.js to upload to GPU
};

colorImage.src = '/textures/wooden_door/Door_Wood_001_basecolor.jpg';
```

The critical detail: **`needsUpdate = true`**. Without it, Three.js won't re-upload the texture data to the GPU, and you'll see a blank/white surface.

#### 2. `THREE.TextureLoader`

A convenience wrapper around the native `Image` approach. Handles the `onload` and `needsUpdate` logic for you:

```ts
const textureLoader = new THREE.TextureLoader();
const colorMap = textureLoader.load(
  '/textures/wooden_door/Door_Wood_001_basecolor.jpg',
  (texture) => { /* loaded */ },
  (progress) => { /* progress */ },
  (error) => { /* error */ },
);
```

One `TextureLoader` instance can load multiple textures — it's reusable.

#### 3. `THREE.LoadingManager` + `TextureLoader`

When you need to track the global loading progress (e.g. show a loading bar for all textures), use a `LoadingManager`:

```ts
const loadingManager = new THREE.LoadingManager();
const textureLoader = new THREE.TextureLoader(loadingManager);

const colorMap = textureLoader.load('/textures/wooden_door/Door_Wood_001_basecolor.jpg');
const normalMap = textureLoader.load('/textures/wooden_door/Door_Wood_001_normal.jpg');

loadingManager.onStart = (url) => { console.log('loading started', url); };
loadingManager.onLoad = () => { console.log('all loaded'); };
loadingManager.onProgress = (url, loaded, total) => { console.log(`${loaded}/${total}`); };
loadingManager.onError = (url) => { console.error('failed', url); };
```

The `LoadingManager` consolidates events from all loaders into one place — you get a single `onLoad` when everything is done, instead of one callback per texture.

**Note:** `onStart` may not fire if the textures are already cached by the browser. This is normal browser behavior, not a bug.

## Code

in repo - `apps/8_textures`

```ts
import * as THREE from 'three/webgpu';
import { OrbitControls } from 'three/examples/jsm/Addons.js';

// --- Loading textures ---

// Way 1: Native Image + Texture (what happens under the hood)
const colorImage = new Image();
const colorTexture = new THREE.Texture(colorImage);

colorImage.onload = () => {
  colorTexture.needsUpdate = true;
};
colorImage.src = '/textures/wooden_door/Door_Wood_001_basecolor.jpg';

// Way 2: TextureLoader (convenience wrapper)
const loadingManager = new THREE.LoadingManager();
const textureLoader = new THREE.TextureLoader(loadingManager);
const colorMap = textureLoader.load('/textures/wooden_door/Door_Wood_001_basecolor.jpg');

// LoadingManager events — track global progress
loadingManager.onStart = (path) => { console.log('loading started'); };
loadingManager.onLoad = () => { console.log('loading finished'); };
loadingManager.onProgress = (path) => { console.log('loading progressing'); };
loadingManager.onError = (e) => { console.error('Loading failed', e); };

// --- Scene setup ---

const scene = new THREE.Scene();
const geometry = new THREE.BoxGeometry(1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2);

const material = new THREE.MeshBasicMaterial({
  map: colorMap,  // apply the color texture
});

const boxMesh = new THREE.Mesh(geometry, material);
scene.add(boxMesh);

const camera = new THREE.PerspectiveCamera(75, innerWidth / innerHeight, 0.1, 100);
camera.position.set(1, 1.5, 3);
camera.lookAt(boxMesh.position);
scene.add(camera);

const canvas = document.querySelector<HTMLCanvasElement>('canvas#tride')!;
const controls = new OrbitControls(camera, canvas);
controls.enableDamping = true;

const renderer = new THREE.WebGPURenderer({ canvas });
await renderer.init();
renderer.setPixelRatio(Math.min(devicePixelRatio, 2));
renderer.setSize(innerWidth, innerHeight);

function tick() {
  controls.update();
  renderer.render(scene, camera);
  requestAnimationFrame(tick);
}
tick();
```

## Gotchas

- **`needsUpdate = true` is mandatory** — When loading a texture manually with `new Image()`, you must set `texture.needsUpdate = true` after the image loads. Without it, Three.js doesn't upload the image data to the GPU, and the texture appears blank.

- **Alpha maps need `transparent: true`** — An alpha map alone won't make anything transparent. The material must also have `transparent: true` set. Forgetting this is the #1 alpha map gotcha.

- **Height maps need enough geometry** — A `BoxGeometry(1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1)` (1 segment per face) has only 2 triangles per face — not enough vertices to displace. For displacement to look good, you need a high-segment-count geometry. Normal maps don't have this limitation.

- **Normal maps look purple — that's normal** — The blue-purple appearance is by design. The RGB channels encode the XYZ of the surface normal vector. Since normals mostly point outward (toward the viewer), the blue channel dominates.

- **Metalness and Roughness go together** — Metalness controls "is this metal?" and Roughness controls "how smooth is this surface?" They work as a pair. In the real world, metals are always smooth-ish (metalness=1, roughness varies), while non-metals (wood, plastic, fabric) are always non-metallic (metalness=0, roughness varies). Mixing high metalness with high roughness creates materials that don't exist in nature.

- **Texture files live in `public/`, not `src/assets/`** — When loading textures at runtime (not at build time), put them in `public/` and reference them with a path starting from `/`. Vite serves `public/` as the root. Files in `src/assets/` are handled by Vite's bundler — they work too, but the runtime loading approach is more explicit.

- **`onStart` may not fire** — If textures are cached by the browser, `LoadingManager.onStart` might not trigger. This is normal browser caching behavior, not a bug in your code.

## Revisit

- **Other texture types** — Environment maps (for reflections), emissive maps (for glow), and light maps (for baked lighting) are worth studying when you get to those topics. This lesson only covers the most common PBR maps.
- **Texture repeat and wrap modes** — `THREE.RepeatWrapping` lets you tile textures across a surface. Useful for floor tiles, brick walls, and other repeating patterns. Not covered here.
- **Texture filtering** — `minFilter` and `magFilter` control how textures look when zoomed in/out. The default is fine for most cases, but `THREE.NearestFilter` gives a pixelated retro look.
- **Normal map strength** — The `normalScale` property lets you control how strong the normal map effect is. Useful for dialing back the effect if it looks too intense.
- **PBR deeper dive** — The metalness/roughness workflow is the most common PBR model, but there's also a **specular/glossiness** workflow used in some engines. Worth knowing it exists.

## Outdated

The approach is valid. The texture loading APIs and PBR material system in Three.js are stable and actively maintained. No major deprecations to worry about.

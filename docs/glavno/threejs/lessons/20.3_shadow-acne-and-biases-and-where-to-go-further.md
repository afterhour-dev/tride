# Shadow Acne, Bias, and NormalBias in Three.js

I peronally never encounter this with my models which I built in blender but I saw model built with blender (a hamburger or sandwich model), that is more curvy and it has this acne on its surface.

## What Shadow Acne Is

Shadow mapping works by rendering the scene from the light's point of view into a depth texture (the "shadow map"), then later comparing each pixel's depth-from-light against that stored value to decide whether it's lit or in shadow.

The depth texture has finite precision, and that precision is quantized across the light's view frustum. On surfaces where light hits at a grazing/shallow angle relative to the surface normal, tiny quantization errors cause the surface to incorrectly test as "behind itself" in spots. This produces a speckled, noisy self-shadowing pattern known as **shadow acne**.

This is a fundamental artifact of how shadow mapping works — it shows up the same way regardless of renderer backend (WebGL or WebGPU), since both use the same underlying shadow-mapping technique in Three.js.

## Why Some Models Show It More Than Others

Acne severity comes down to geometry and shadow camera setup, not the rendering API:

- **Flat vs. curved surfaces**: Blocky, hard-surface models (e.g. a Game Boy-like console) are mostly flat panels facing the light head-on, with little depth-precision ambiguity — acne rarely appears.
- **Curved/organic geometry**: Models with continuously curving normals (a burger, organic shapes, character models) sweep through many grazing angles relative to the light, which is exactly where shadow-map depth comparisons get most sensitive to precision error. These are classic acne magnets.
- **Shadow camera fit**: A loosely fit shadow camera frustum (common with default settings on an arbitrary import) wastes depth precision and worsens acne. Model scale and distance from the shadow camera's near/far planes matter too.

## The Fix: `bias` vs `normalBias`

Both properties live on `light.shadow` and correct for shadow acne, but they act differently:

- **`bias`**: Shifts the *compared depth value* itself, along the light's view/depth axis. It's a uniform push in "distance from light" space, applied the same everywhere regardless of surface orientation.
- **`normalBias`**: Shifts the *sample position* outward along the surface normal before the depth lookup. The correction scales with how steeply the normal is angled relative to the light — it naturally compensates more where the angle is worse and less where it isn't.

### Why `normalBias` tends to suit curved geometry better

Acne is worst at grazing angles. Curved models have a continuous range of normals sweeping through those angles somewhere on the surface, and `normalBias` adapts its correction across that range. A flat `bias` offset can't adapt — you'd need to crank it uniformly high enough to fix the worst grazing spots, which then over-corrects flat/head-on areas and causes **peter-panning** (shadows visibly detaching from their objects) there.

On blocky models, most faces are either near-perpendicular to the light or in full light/shadow with sharp transitions, so there isn't much of a grazing-angle gradient for `normalBias` to specifically help with — plain `bias` is usually sufficient there.

In practice, most scenes benefit from tuning **both together**.

## Typical Starting Ranges

| Property | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `bias` | `-0.005` to `0.005` (often `-0.001` to `0.0001`) | Small, usually negative |
| `normalBias` | `0` to `0.2` (often `0.02`–`0.05` for curved geometry) | Scales with surface curvature |

## Tuning Live with a GUI

Because the correct values depend on scene scale, geometry, and shadow map resolution, tuning with a debug GUI (e.g. `lil-gui`) is the standard workflow rather than guessing fixed values.

```javascript
import GUI from 'lil-gui';

const gui = new GUI();
const shadowFolder = gui.addFolder('Shadow Tuning');

shadowFolder.add(light.shadow, 'bias', -0.005, 0.005, 0.0001);
shadowFolder.add(light.shadow, 'normalBias', 0, 0.2, 0.001);
shadowFolder.add(light.shadow.mapSize, 'width', [512, 1024, 2048, 4096])
  .onChange(() => {
    light.shadow.mapSize.height = light.shadow.mapSize.width;
    light.shadow.map?.dispose();
    light.shadow.map = null; // forces regeneration at new size
  });
```

### What to Watch For

- **Too little bias** → speckled acne/moiré patterns remain visible on the surface.
- **Too much bias** → peter-panning: the shadow visibly detaches from the object, floating away from its base — most noticeable where an object touches the ground.
- **Sweet spot**: the smallest bias value that just barely eliminates the acne. Push it as little as needed, then stop.

## Practical Tips

1. **Tighten the shadow camera first.** Fit `shadow.camera.near/far` and the frustum bounds to the scene's actual dimensions *before* tuning bias. A poorly fit shadow camera wastes depth precision, forcing you to use larger (worse) bias values to compensate.
2. **Increase `mapSize` if needed.** Bumping `light.shadow.mapSize.width/height` (e.g. to 2048) improves depth precision and reduces how much bias correction is required.
3. **Tune at multiple angles.** Acne severity can shift with viewing angle even though the shadow map itself doesn't change — check a few camera positions while tuning.
4. **Tune with your final shadow map type.** Soft shadow types like `PCFSoftShadowMap` can mask or interact with acne differently than hard shadow maps, so tune bias with the actual type you'll ship.

## Key Takeaway

Shadow acne is a geometry- and shadow-camera-setup issue inherent to shadow mapping, not a WebGL-vs-WebGPU distinction. Flat, blocky models are naturally more forgiving; curved, organic models expose the underlying precision problem more readily. `bias` and `normalBias` are complementary correction tools — `normalBias` is generally the more effective first lever for curved geometry, while a small `bias` handles the rest — with a debug GUI as the practical way to find the right combination for a given scene.

# Going Ffurther with realistic render

Next are all post-processing / rendering techniques that layer on top of the fundamentals you've already covered (tone mapping, shadows, color space, AA (antialiasing)). Here's a quick rundown of each:

**Ambient Occlusion (AO)**  
Darkens areas where surfaces are close together or crevices block ambient light from reaching them — corners, cracks, where objects meet the ground. Without it, contact points look like they're "floating" because there's no soft contact shadow. In Three.js this is typically a post-process pass (SSAO, or the newer GTAO/N8AO) sampling nearby depth/normals to estimate occlusion per pixel.

**Depth of Field (DoF)**  
Simulates a camera lens — objects at the focus distance are sharp, everything nearer or farther gets progressively blurrier. Adds photographic realism and directs the viewer's eye. Driven by a focus distance, focal length, and aperture (f-stop); wider aperture = shallower focus = more blur.

**Motion Blur**  
Blurs fast-moving objects (or the whole frame during fast camera movement) the way a real camera shutter would smear motion over its exposure time. Usually computed from per-pixel velocity vectors (how much a pixel moved since the last frame) rather than literally re-rendering multiple frames.

**Bloom**  
Makes very bright areas "glow" and bleed light into surrounding pixels, mimicking how camera lenses and human eyes respond to intense light. Works by extracting pixels above a brightness threshold, blurring that extracted layer, and additively blending it back over the original image. Pairs closely with HDR + tone mapping since bloom needs values above 1.0 to know what counts as "bright."

**Fog**  
Fades objects toward a fog color as they get farther from the camera, simulating atmospheric scattering (haze, mist, distance haze). Three.js has this built in cheaply (`THREE.Fog` / `THREE.FogExp2`) as a distance-based color blend, separate from full volumetric fog which is a heavier post-process/raymarching technique. (I already tried `THREE.Fog` once)

**Vignette**  
Darkens (or sometimes desaturates) the corners of the frame relative to the center, mimicking light falloff in real camera lenses. Purely a framing/compositional effect — no physical simulation, just a radial darkening gradient applied to the final image. Cheap and purely aesthetic.

**Bokeh**  
Technically the _character_ of the out-of-focus blur produced by DoF — the shape (hexagonal, circular, etc.) that blurred bright points of light take on, based on the lens aperture's shape. "Bokeh" in Three.js examples/passes often just refers to a specific DoF implementation (`BokehPass`), so you'll frequently see DoF and bokeh used almost interchangeably, though bokeh technically describes the aesthetic quality, not the effect itself.

**A few more worth knowing about, since you asked for "and there are more":**

- **Chromatic Aberration** — slight color fringing/separation (RGB channels offset) toward the edges of the frame, mimicking lens imperfections.
- **Film Grain / Noise** — adds subtle grain to avoid an overly "clean" CG look and can help mask banding.
- **Lens Flare** — bright streaks/rings/artifacts from a light source shining directly into the lens.
- **Color Grading / LUTs** — applying a lookup table to shift overall color/contrast mood, distinct from tone mapping (which handles HDR→LDR compression) though the two are often applied together.
- **Screen Space Reflections (SSR)** — approximate reflections of the visible scene on reflective surfaces, without full ray tracing.
- **Ambient/Environment Lighting via IBL** — using an HDRI environment map to light objects realistically instead of relying purely on direct lights (closely tied to your color space and tone mapping work, since it's usually HDR).

Most of these (AO, DoF, bloom, vignette, chromatic aberration, film grain) are implemented in Three.js via `EffectComposer` and chained render passes — so once you're comfortable with your bias/shadow tuning workflow, plugging these in follows a similar pattern: add a pass, expose its parameters to your GUI, and tune by eye.

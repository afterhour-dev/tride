# 20.0 Realistic Render — Tone Mapping & Antialiasing

## Concept

A realistic render is not one big thing — it's a pile of small settings that each nudge the image toward what a camera or the human eye produces. The previous lessons gave us a **good environment map** (an HDR equirectangular texture from Poly Haven, `19.x`), which provides believable lighting and reflections. This lesson adds two more pieces of that pile: **tone mapping** (how the image's brightness is compressed) and **antialiasing** (how the edges stop looking like stairs).

The app itself is a "set-up": the Flight Helmet (from glTF-Sample-Models), a gaming console modeled in Blender and Draco-compressed, and the `abandoned_garage` HDR environment map — all loaded and tweaked from a lil-gui panel. Everything we actually learn here is the two renderer settings we toggle in that GUI.

### Tone mapping

**Tone mapping converts High Dynamic Range (HDR) values down to Low Dynamic Range (LDR) values** — the `0..1` per-channel range a normal screen can display.

- Light in the real world is **unbounded**: a light source or a specular highlight can be *brighter* than `1.0`, with values far beyond what a monitor can show. If you render such an HDR scene straight to the screen, the bright parts just clip to pure white and you lose all detail.
- **Tone mapping** is the curve that compresses those huge values down into `0..1`, preserving the *look* of brightness (and detail in highlights) instead of hard-clipping.
- In Three.js the trick is that it **fakes the process**: the default `NoToneMapping` also compresses values (the internal pipeline already maps the higher range), so even with ordinary non-HDR colors you get a *realistic-looking* result just by selecting a tone mapping curve. You don't need actual HDR data for tone mapping to visibly improve the image.

The common tone mapping options (all selectable in the GUI):

| Setting | Look |
|---|---|
| `THREE.NoToneMapping` | Default — no tone mapping curve applied, colors just clip. |
| `THREE.LinearToneMapping` | Linear compression. **Looks basically identical to `NoToneMapping`** for ordinary LDR content. |
| `THREE.ReinhardToneMapping` | Colors look **washed out but very realistic**, like a poorly-exposed camera. |
| `THREE.CineonToneMapping` | Cinematic, filmic curve. |
| `THREE.ACESFilmicToneMapping` | Film-like, good highlight roll-off; a very common "correct" look. |

**Why do `NoToneMapping` and `LinearToneMapping` look the same?** Because both are linear mappings — they compress the range proportionally, preserving the straight-line relationship between input and output. For the relatively dark, LDR content of this scene neither adds a curve, so the difference is invisible. The *interesting* operators (`Reinhard`, `Cineon`, `ACESFilmic`) apply a **non-linear curve** that lifts or rolls off tones differently — *that's* what produces the visible change.

**Why only Cineon, Reinhard and ACESFilmic are "popular"?** Because they're the ones with a filmic/camera-like curve. `Linear` is "boring" precisely because it behaves like the default — no personality. The popular three each mimic how film or the eye handles brightness: ACES is the modern "filmic standard", Cineon emulates cineon film scan, Reinhard is a simple and safe curve. In this lesson Reinhard is used most because it's realistic and easy to read. (Three.js also offers `AgXToneMapping` and `NeutralToneMapping` — newer, more physically-accurate options; see Revisit.)

**Set it like this:**
```ts
renderer.toneMapping = THREE.ReinhardToneMapping;
```

### `toneMappingExposure`

Exposure is a **global brightness multiplier applied before tone mapping**:

```ts
renderer.toneMappingExposure = 3;
```

- **What it does:** it scales all the HDR values before the tone mapping curve compresses them — so higher exposure = brighter image. Because it's applied *before* the non-linear curve, changing it does more than just a linear brightness slider: it shifts where the curve operates, changing highlight roll-off, not only overall brightness.
- **Allowed range:** there is **no hard limit** — it's a `float`, you can go below `1` and far above. The GUI here sliders it `1..10` for convenience. Values below `1` darken, above `1` brighten.
- **Does screen luminosity matter?** In a well-calibrated HDR context yes, exposure interacts with how bright your display is — but in practice on a normal monitor it's just a feel knob: pick the value that makes the render look right. There's no "correct" setting without a calibrated HDR display.

### Antialiasing

**Aliasing** is the stair-like (jagged) artifact you see on the edges of geometry — most visible in this app on the console's edges and the goggles of the Flight Helmet, especially with **high contrast** (a bright edge against a dark background).

**Why it happens:** a pixel is a single square. When the GPU renders that pixel it determines which geometry covers it and computes one color for the whole pixel. When a geometry edge falls *between* pixel centers, the pixel gets colored as either fully-on or fully-off, so the edge renders as a hard staircase instead of a smooth line.

**How we fix it — the two classic families:**

1. **Super Sampling / SSAA** (also called Full-Screen AA, FSAA): render the scene at a **higher resolution** than the display, then downscale. Each final pixel's color is the *average* of several rendered sub-pixels, so edges are smoothly blended. Easy to understand, but **expensive** — you're shading N× the pixels. Great for scenes with few lights or simple materials (e.g. matcap-only renders); not ideal for a heavy, realistic scene. Not deprecated as a concept — modern GPUs/upscalers (DLSS, FSR) are built on the same "render higher, blend down" idea — but brute-force SSAA is rarely the best choice today. (See Revisit for modern alternatives.)
2. **Multi-Sampling / MSAA** (what we use here): renders the geometry edges with **multiple color samples per pixel**, then blends those samples together for the final pixel color. It only samples *more* where it matters — on the edges — so it's **cheap** and effective for geometry edges. Most recent GPUs support it natively.

**Important limitation of MSAA:** it only smooths **geometry edges** — it does *not* fix aliasing *inside* a surface (e.g. sharp/high-frequency details from a texture). For texture aliasing you fix it differently — with mipmaps and filtering (`8.3`).

**Enable MSAA on the WebGPURenderer:**
```ts
const renderer = new THREE.WebGPURenderer({
    canvas,
    antialias: true, // enables MSAA (4 samples) if the device supports it
});
```
- `antialias: true` is a shortcut for `renderer.samples = 4` (WebGPU only supports `1` or `4` samples; the WebGL backend supports up to ~8).
- It slightly uses more resources, and **screens with `devicePixelRatio > 1` barely need it** — high-DPI screens already pack enough pixels per inch that edges look smooth.

## Code

in repo - `apps/20_0_realistic_render_with_tonemapping_and_antialiasing`

```ts
import * as THREE from 'three/webgpu';

const renderer = new THREE.WebGPURenderer({
    canvas,
    // MSAA — smooths geometry edges. Note: WebGPU only supports 1 or 4 samples.
    antialias: true,
});

// tone mapping — HDR → LDR curve. Reinhard = washed-out but realistic.
renderer.toneMapping = THREE.ReinhardToneMapping;

// exposure — global brightness applied BEFORE the tone mapping curve.
renderer.toneMappingExposure = 3;

await renderer.init();

renderer.setPixelRatio(Math.min(window.devicePixelRatio, 2));
renderer.setSize(window.innerWidth, window.innerHeight);

// ... load models + HDR environment map, build scene, setAnimationLoop(tick)
```

## Gotchas

- **`NoToneMapping` and `LinearToneMapping` look the same — that's expected.** Both are linear; for ordinary LDR content neither adds a visible curve. Don't waste time hunting for a difference — pick a non-linear operator (`Reinhard`/`Cineon`/`ACESFilmic`) to actually see tone mapping do something.
- **`toneMappingExposure` can go below `1`** (darken) — the GUI's `1..10` slider is just a convenience range, not the legal range. It's a plain float with no hard bounds.
- **MSAA only fixes geometry edges, not texture aliasing.** Sharp, high-frequency detail *inside* a surface still shimmers/stairs — fix that with mipmaps and `minFilter` (`8.3`), not `antialias`. The README explicitly calls this out.
- **`antialias: true` is a shortcut for `samples = 4`, and WebGPU only allows `1` or `4`.** Don't set `samples: 8` expecting more — WebGPU rejects it (the WebGL backend allows up to ~8).
- **High-DPI screens don't need `antialias`.** If `window.devicePixelRatio > 1` the extra pixel density already smooths edges; enabling MSAA there is wasted resources. The README's idea — `Math.min(window.devicePixelRatio, 2) === 1 ? true : false` — is a legit way to enable it only where it helps.
- **This app is WebGPU.** It imports from `three/webgpu` and uses `THREE.WebGPURenderer`; `antialias`/`samples` as renderer options behave as documented for WebGPU (see Revisit for the WebGL backend differences).
- **Tone mapping is a global renderer setting** (unlike per-material props) — changing it in the GUI restyles the entire frame at once, which is why flipping values produces such a dramatic whole-scene change.

## Revisit

- **The `NoToneMapping` vs `LinearToneMapping` question.** It's tempting to think one is "broken" — it isn't; they're just both linear. If you want to *see* tone mapping, use a non-linear operator. Worth re-testing with a genuinely bright/HDR source (a bright emissive object, or a high-exposure setting) to see the highlight roll-off difference.
- **SSAA: deprecated or not?** Not deprecated as a concept, but brute-force SSAA is rarely the best modern choice. The descendant of the idea — render at higher resolution and intelligently downscale — lives on in **DLSS/FSR/XeSS** and in post-processing AA. For real-time WebGPU, the modern path is **TRAA** (temporal anti-aliasing, part of the newer TSL post-processing stack) or **FXAA/SMAA** post-pass. Worth experimenting with as a next step.
- **MSAA on the AMD Lucienne (integrated) GPU.** The README asks whether MSAA works on an iGPU — it does, since `antialias: true` falls back gracefully when the device doesn't support multisampling. Confirm your own device's behavior with the `webgpu_multisampled_renderbuffers` example.
- **The `devicePixelRatio`-conditional antialias.** The README's `Math.min(window.devicePixelRatio, 2) === 1 ? true : false` idea is sound — consider baking it in so you only pay for MSAA when the screen is low-DPI.
- **Newer tone mapping operators.** `THREE.AgXToneMapping` and `THREE.NeutralToneMapping` are more modern, more physically-accurate curves (the tonemapping official example already lists them) — try them and compare against Reinhard/Cineon/ACESFilmic.
- **WebGL vs WebGPU antialias differences.** On the WebGL backend `samples` supports up to ~8, and MSAA+postprocessing interplay differs. If you ever drop back to `WebGLRenderer`, re-test the sample counts.
- **Tone mapping + HDR output is still evolving** (see the linked HDR PR) — if you move to an `outputType: HalfFloatType` / HDR workflow, tone mapping and HDR "cannot fully coexist yet" in some configs. Keep an eye on it.

## Outdated

approach is valid

> 💡 **This is a WebGPU-first lesson.** Tone mapping, `toneMappingExposure` and `antialias: true`/`samples` are set on `THREE.WebGPURenderer` (`three/webgpu`). The classic `WebGLRenderer` also supports tone mapping and antialias, but with different sample-count limits (up to ~8) and different MSAA/postprocessing interplay. The modern WebGPU post-processing stack (TSL `pass()`/`renderOutput()`) applies tone mapping automatically at the end of the chain and offers **TRAA** as a more advanced AA than MSAA.

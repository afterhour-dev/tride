# Spot Light

Exploring `SpotLight` — the three.js light that behaves like a **flashlight**: a cone of light that starts at a single point and is aimed along one direction.

## Concept

A `SpotLight` is the closest Three.js equivalent of a **flashlight** or **torch** (or a stage spotlight, a theater light, a car headlight). If you hold a flashlight, the bulb is one point in space, the beam points in a specific **direction**, and the light comes out as a **cone** that widens as it travels. That's exactly what `SpotLight` models.

To picture it, break it into three pieces:

1. **A source point** — the light has a `position` in the scene, like a flashlight bulb. `spotLight.position.set(0, 2, 3)` puts it up and in front of the objects.
2. **A direction it aims along** — the beam points somewhere. In Three.js the direction is *not* set directly with `rotation` (see Gotchas); it's set with the light's **`target`** — an `Object3D` that the light always faces. A `SpotLight` and a `TargetLight` example: the light shines **from its position toward its target**.
3. **A cone of light** — unlike a `PointLight` (which radiates `360°` like a bare bulb), a spotlight's rays are confined to a cone. Near the center of the cone it's bright; at the edges it falls off (how soft that edge is — called `penumbra` — you control).

That "cone aimed at a point" model is the key mental image, and it's the property that makes a spotlight *directional-local*: it's localized at a point (like a point light) but only lights a limited, aimed region (unlike a point light).

### The constructor arguments

```ts
new THREE.SpotLight(color, intensity, distance, angle, penumbra, decay)
```

- **`color`** — the light's color (default `0xffffff`). Here `0x78ff00`, a bright green.
- **`intensity`** — brightness (default `1`). Here `0.5`.
- **`distance`** — (optional) *maximum* range in world units. Light only reaches surfaces within this radius; beyond it, nothing. `0` = unlimited. Here `10`.
- **`angle`** — (optional) the **half-aperture** (half-angle) of the cone, in radians, from the axis to one edge. Default `PI/3`. Here `Math.PI * 0.1` — a tight beam. This is a **half** angle: the total cone spans twice this.
- **`penumbra`** — (optional) how **soft** the edge of the cone is, `0` to `1`. `0` = a hard, sharp cut-off edge at the cone boundary; `1` = fully soft, fading gradually to nothing across the whole cone. Here `0.25`.
- **`decay`** — (optional) how fast the light falls off with distance. Here `1` (linear falloff).

You can pass all six at construction, or set them afterwards (as the app does) so they can be tweaked live in a lil-gui panel:

```ts
const spotLight = new THREE.SpotLight();
spotLight.color = new THREE.Color(0x78ff00);
spotLight.intensity = 0.5;
spotLight.distance = 10;
spotLight.angle = Math.PI * 0.1;
spotLight.penumbra = 0.25;
spotLight.decay = 1;
spotLight.position.set(0, 2, 3);
scene.add(spotLight);
```

### `angle` — how wide the cone is

`angle` is the **half-angle** of the cone, in **radians**, measured from the beam's central axis out to one edge. So the full cone spans `2 × angle`.

- Small `angle` (like `Math.PI * 0.1` ≈ 0.31 rad ≈ 18°) → a **narrow, focused** beam, like a tight flashlight.
- Large `angle` (up to `PI/2`) → a **wide** cone.

Because it's in radians, `Math.PI * 0.1` is a natural way to get a small, precise value. The app exposes `angle` in the GUI from `0` to `2 * Math.PI`, so you can widen it until the cone swallows the whole scene and back down to a pin-prick beam.

### `penumbra` — the strange name, explained

`penumbra` sounds odd, but it comes from astronomy: the **penumbra** of a shadow is the *soft, partially-lit fringe* around the dark **umbra** (the fully-shadowed core). Three.js borrows the word for the cone's edge.

It's a `0`–`1` value controlling how **sharp or diffuse** the boundary of the lit cone is:

- `penumbra = 0` → a **hard edge**: fully lit inside the cone boundary, pitch black just outside it. Looks artificial, like a crisp cut-out.
- `penumbra = 1` → a **completely soft edge**: the light fades smoothly from full brightness at the axis to nothing at the cone boundary — no visible seam.
- In between (`0.25` here) → a partially soft edge, with a narrow partially-bright fringe.

Think of it as the "feather" of the spotlight. It only affects the **cone boundary**, not the overall distance falloff (that's `decay`).

### `decay` — distance falloff

`decay` controls how quickly brightness drops as light travels from the source:

- `0` → no fade with distance.
- `1` → linear falloff (`1/d`). This is what the app uses.
- `2` → physically correct quadratic falloff (`1/d²`).

In the app `decay = 1`, giving a gentle linear falloff that keeps the whole small scene reasonably lit. As with other lights, when `useLegacyLights = false` (the modern WebGPU default) intensity/decay behave physically.

### The `target` — how the spotlight aims

The cone doesn't just point "up" by default — it aims at its **`target`**, an `Object3D`. The light always points **from its `position` toward its `target`'s position**. This is the piece that differs most from the earlier light lessons, and it's what the app experiments with.

Every `SpotLight` already owns a default `target` (an empty `Object3D` sitting at the origin). Crucially **the default target is not added to the scene**, so:

- If you want to **move** that empty target to aim the light, you must add it to the scene first: `scene.add(spotLight.target)`. Then moving `spotLight.target.position` re-aims the cone. (An object not in the scene still updates its transform in its own local space, but it is not visited by the scene graph — this code comment notes that the GUI target tweaks are commented out precisely because the target wasn't `scene.add`-ed.)
- Alternatively, you can **aim at an object that's already in the scene** by reassigning the target: `spotLight.target = sphereMesh`. Then the cone always points at the sphere, and if you animate the sphere, the light follows it automatically.

```ts
// Aim the spotlight at an existing mesh — it follows automatically
spotLight.target = sphereMesh;

// Or keep the built-in empty target, and add it to the scene so it can be moved
scene.add(spotLight.target);
spotLight.target.position.x = 0.4; // re-aims the cone
```

Because `target` is just an `Object3D`, you can also **swap targets** at runtime: make a mesh the target, then later point back at the original empty `Object3D`, or another object entirely. The GUI buttons `makeSphereTarget`, `makeRandomTarget`, and `removeTarget` demonstrate exactly this switching.

> Note: `spotLight.lookAt(sphereMesh.position)` (tried in the README) does **not** reliably re-aim the light the same way the `target` does — in the Three.js WebGPU path a spotlight's orientation is driven by its `target`, not by its own rotation. Use the `target` to aim.

## Code

in repo - `apps/12_6_spot_light`

```ts
import * as THREE from 'three/webgpu';

const canvas = document.querySelector<HTMLCanvasElement>('canvas#tride')!;

async function init() {
  const scene = new THREE.Scene();

  const renderer = new THREE.WebGPURenderer({ canvas });
  await renderer.init();

  // SpotLight — like a flashlight: a cone aimed at a target.
  const spotLight = new THREE.SpotLight();
  spotLight.color = new THREE.Color(0x78ff00);
  spotLight.intensity = 0.5;
  spotLight.distance = 10;          // max range in world units
  spotLight.angle = Math.PI * 0.1;  // cone half-angle, in radians (tight beam)
  spotLight.penumbra = 0.25;        // 0 = hard edge, 1 = fully soft edge
  spotLight.decay = 1;              // linear falloff with distance
  spotLight.position.set(0, 2, 3);  // place the "flashlight"
  scene.add(spotLight);

  // Aim the cone at a mesh already in the scene — light follows it.
  // (To move the built-in empty target instead, add it: scene.add(spotLight.target))
  spotLight.target = sphereMesh;

  // MeshStandardMaterial needs light to be visible
  const material = new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial();
  material.roughness = 0.4;

  const box = new THREE.Mesh(new THREE.BoxGeometry(0.75, 0.75, 0.75), material);
  const torus = new THREE.Mesh(new THREE.TorusGeometry(0.3, 0.2, 32, 64), material);
  torus.position.x = 1.5;
  const sphere = new THREE.Mesh(new THREE.SphereGeometry(0.5, 32, 32), material);
  sphere.position.x = -1.5;
  const floor = new THREE.Mesh(new THREE.PlaneGeometry(5, 5), material);
  floor.rotation.x = -Math.PI / 2;
  floor.position.y = -0.65;

  scene.add(box, torus, sphere, floor);

  // Camera
  const camera = new THREE.PerspectiveCamera(75, innerWidth / innerHeight, 0.1, 100);
  camera.position.set(2, 1, 1);
  scene.add(camera);

  // Render loop
  renderer.setPixelRatio(Math.min(devicePixelRatio, 2));
  renderer.setSize(innerWidth, innerHeight);
  renderer.setAnimationLoop(() => {
    renderer.render(scene, camera);
  });
}

await init();
```

> In the real app, `color`, `intensity`, `distance`, `angle`, `penumbra`, `decay`, `position`, and `visible`, plus the `makeSphereTarget` / `makeRandomTarget` / `removeTarget` target-switching buttons, are all exposed to a lil-gui "Spot Light tweaks" folder so the beam can be explored live.

## Gotchas

- **A spotlight's direction comes from its `target`, not its `rotation`.** The app even labels the `rotation.x` GUI slider "(no effect)" — rotating the light itself does not re-aim the beam in the WebGPU path. To aim a `SpotLight`, move its `target` (and make sure the target is positioned correctly); `lookAt()` does not reliably replace this.

- **The default `target` must be added to the scene before you can move it.** Every `SpotLight` ships with a built-in empty `Object3D` target sitting at the origin, but it is *not* part of the scene graph. If you set `spotLight.target.position.x = ...` without `scene.add(spotLight.target)`, the light doesn't visibly re-aim — the object isn't being updated in the scene. This is why the app comments out its target GUI tweaks until a target-switch button adds it.

- **`angle` is a *half*-angle, in radians.** The visible cone spans `2 × angle`. Saying "the cone is `Math.PI * 0.1` wide" would be wrong — it's `Math.PI * 0.1` per side. And it's radians, not degrees, so pass `Math.PI * x`, not decimal degrees.

- **`penumbra` only softens the cone's *edge*, not the whole distance falloff.** `0` = harsh cut-off at the cone boundary; `1` = fully feathered. Mixing it up with `decay` (distance falloff) is easy — they're independent.

- **`RectAreaLightNode.setLTC` is only needed for `RectAreaLight`.** The app comments out `THREE.RectAreaLightNode.setLTC(RectAreaLightTexturesLib.init())` and notes that no *other* light requires it. This LTC look-up-table init is specific to area-light shading; a `SpotLight` doesn't need it. Don't cargo-cult that line into spotlight-only scenes.

- **`MeshStandardMaterial` renders black without light.** As in the other lessons, all the other lights (`Ambient`, `Directional`, `Hemisphere`, `Point`) are created but set to `visible = false`, so the spotlight's own cone is the only thing lighting the surfaces. Toggle the spotlight off and the scene goes dark.

- **`await renderer.init()` is mandatory for WebGPU.** The `WebGPURenderer` must finish async initialization before the first render, or you get a blank canvas.

## Revisit

- **`decay` for spotlights.** This app uses `decay = 1` (linear) and clamps the GUI to `0–1`. Revisit with `decay = 2` and physically correct `1/d²` falloff to feel the difference between a gentle, far-reaching beam and a sharp, short-range one. Combined with `distance`, these control the whole reach of the beam.

- **`penumbra` values against a real cone.** Sweep `penumbra` from `0` to `1` with a wide `angle` to really internalize "umbra vs penumbra" — the astronomy analogy is worth anchoring visually.

- **Targets as a general `Object3D` mechanism.** The app swaps between `sphereMesh` and the built-in empty target and can even instantiate a fresh `Object3D`, assign it as the target, and switch between aim points at runtime. This is a general pattern (any `Object3D` can be a target) worth reusing for e.g. an AI flashlight that chases a moving player.

- **Shadows.** `SpotLight` is one of the light types that *can* cast shadows (with a cone-shaped shadow camera). Revisit once you reach the shadows lesson — spotlights are a common choice for shadow casting because their frustum matches their cone.

## Outdated

Approach is valid — this is a current WebGPU-based Three.js setup using the recommended `WebGPURenderer` and PBR (`MeshStandardMaterial`). `SpotLight` remains a standard, fully supported light type. Nothing here is obsolete, and no legacy-light flags need toggling in the modern WebGPU path.

## Concept

The intent in one line: *"do the lighting inside the fragment shader, not with the scene's light objects."* This is the first lesson of the 21_x series that stops treating the fragment shader as a scratch plane — it now shades a **real Blender-exported model** (a monkey) using a `THREE.ShaderMaterial` with a vertex shader and a fragment shader. The technique covered is **ambient lighting** and its slightly better cousin, **hemisphere (hemi) lighting**, both implemented by hand in GLSL.

In the engine (`three.js`), the same two concepts exist as real light objects — `AmbientLight` ([[12.1_ambient-light]]) and `HemisphereLight` ([[12.3_hemisphere-light]]). Here both `THREE.AmbientLight` and `THREE.DirectionalLight` **exist in the scene but are `visible = false`**: the whole point of the lesson is to re-implement that lighting by hand, in the shader, and see exactly what it's made of.

### The stage

A few things make this app different from the flat-plane shader lessons before it:

- **A real model.** `GLTFLoader` loads `/models/monkey/monkey.glb` (exported from Blender). The shader material is assigned to every mesh of the model, and `castShadow = true` is set (shadow infrastructure is pre-configured for upcoming lessons; the floor that would receive the shadows is even there, just `visible = false`).
- **A cube map as `background`, not `environment`.** The skys cube map (`/textures/environmentMaps/skys/`) is loaded and set as `scene.background` — it draws the skybox around the camera. The comment `// scene.environment = ...` stays commented out on purpose: `environment` would apply **environment-map lighting** to materials, and the user explicitly didn't want that kind of lighting interfering while we learn to light by hand in the shader. That distinction (background = picture, environment = light source) matters later.
- **Both scene lights off.** The ambient and directional lights are added to the scene but hidden. Any visible light from the engine would light the *standard* materials (like the floor), while the *shader* materials ignore them entirely — you'd get an inconsistent scene, so they're disabled.

### The backbone: two `vec3`s and a Hadamard product

Everything in the fragment shader hangs off this structure:

```glsl
vec3 baseColor = vec3(.5);   // the paint: what color the surface is
vec3 lighting = vec3(0.);    // the light: how much light reaches the surface
vec3 color = baseColor * lighting;   // paint multiplied by light, channel by channel
```

`color = baseColor * lighting` is a **Hadamard (component-wise) product**: each channel of the paint is scaled by the same channel of the light. Think of it as colored light hitting colored paint — a red light on a blue surface lets no blue through. It's also a filter: lighting can't brighten a channel above what `baseColor` allows, and a lighting channel of `0` kills that color entirely. This multiply-out-a-sum pattern (`Σ lights`, then one multiply by the paint) is the skeleton of *every* shader lighting model you'll write.

### Ambient light — the cheapest thing that counts as light

```glsl
vec3 ambient = vec3(.5);
```

Ambient light is **directionless**: one constant color applied to every surface regardless of orientation. It's the crudest possible approximation of "global illumination" — think of it as the light that has bounced around so much it no longer has a direction. It's cheap because it depends on nothing: no normals, no positions, no angles.

The README's observation nails its visible effect: *when you turn only ambient on, the model's color changes, but **nothing about the model's form shows up***. And of course — the model is a uniform gray and the light is uniform gray, so every pixel gets the same product. There's no signal anywhere that says "this face points up, that one points sideways". Ambient alone is what a silhouette looks like: you know something is there, but not what it's made of. That's the whole reason you need a *directional* term to see form.

### Hemisphere (hemi) light — two ambient lights pretending to be a sky and a ground

```glsl
vec3 skyColor    = vec3(0., .3, .6);   // a bluish "sky" above
vec3 groundColor = vec3(.6, .3, .1);   // a brownish "ground" below

float hemiFraction = remap(normal.y, -1., 1., 0., 1.);
vec3 hemi = mix(groundColor, skyColor, hemiFraction);
```

Hemi lighting is "like two ambient lights": one color comes **from the sky (up)**, another from **the ground (down)** — a crude approximation of bounced/global illumination that at least remembers there is an up and a down. Surfaces facing **up** get the sky color, surfaces facing **down** get the ground color, and everything in between gets a smooth blend based on how much the surface tilts. In the app you see the brownish ground tint creep up from below and bluish sky tint fall from above, and — unlike with pure ambient — the model's features suddenly read: two adjacent faces with different tilts get different colors, and that gradient is what form looks like.

The tilt signal is the surface normal's **Y component** (`normal.y ∈ [-1, 1]`), pushed through the familiar `remap`/`mix` pair from [[0.18_inverse-lerp-and-remap]]: `remap(-1 → 0, +1 → 1)`, then `mix(ground, sky, t)`. `t = 0` (facing straight down) → ground color; `t = 1` (facing straight up) → sky color; `t = 0.5` (vertical wall) → exactly half-and-half. This is the shader-side re-implementation of what the engine's `HemisphereLight` does with its `color` (sky) and `groundColor` properties — described in [[12.3_hemisphere-light]].

This is also what the README means by *"to make hemi light work you need to have two lights"*: the whole trick is the **two-color gradient**. Collapse either term and hemi degenerates back into a flat ambient tint — one uniform color again, no form.

### Getting the normal into the fragment — the vertex shader's job

The fragment's fancier lighting needs a normal, and normals are per-vertex data the GPU owns. They arrive the same way UVs arrived in the earlier lessons: through a **`varying`**, declared in *both* shaders and written by the vertex shader:

```glsl
// shader.vert
varying vec3 vNormal;

void main() {
  vec4 localPosition = vec4(position, 1.);
  gl_Position = projectionMatrix * modelViewMatrix * localPosition;
  vUvs = uv;
  vNormal = (modelMatrix * vec4(normal, 0.)).xyz;
}
```

The vertex shader reads the built-in `normal` varying attribute (the model's per-vertex normal, in **local/model space**) and transforms it into **world space** before the fragment ever sees it:

- `modelMatrix` moves the model from its local space into the scene (this is where the monkey's position/rotation/scale live).
- `vec4(normal, 0.)` uses **`w = 0.0`** on purpose: a normal is a *direction*, not a point, so it must be transformed by the matrix's rotation alone — translation must not move it. (A point would use `w = 1.0`.)
- `.xyz` keeps the resulting `vec4`'s first three components.

Why bother transforming? The fragment shader's job is lighting, and lighting happens in *world space* — "which way does this surface face, relative to the world?" If the model were rotated, an untransformed local normal would describe the wrong world direction. The README flags this as a theme: *"you need to decide the space you do your lighting in"* — local, world, view, clip — and shader lessons later in the series revisit it in detail.

### Why re-normalize the normal in the fragment shader? (the README's question)

A fragment shader runs **once per pixel**, but varyings are only computed at **vertices** (in the vertex shader). In between, the GPU hands the fragment an **interpolated** value: the per-vertex values are blended linearly across the triangle's interior. That interpolation is the catch the README asks you to think about:

Two neighboring vertices have unit-length normals (that's what normals are, by definition: length 1). But the linear interpolation of two unit vectors is **not** unit length in the middle — it dips below 1. For unit vectors `u` and `v`:

$$ |(1 - t)\,u + t\,v|^2 = (1-t)^2 + t^2 + 2t(1-t)\,(u \cdot v) \le 1 $$

(equality only at the endpoints). The sharper the corner, the smaller `u · v` is, and the deeper the dip. And nothing about this is "proper" interpolation — the true surface between the vertices is curved, but the GPU blends the normals straight across the flat triangle in screen space.

So the fragment's `vec3 normal = normalize(vNormal);` **restores the unit length** that vertex sampling + interpolation destroyed. It matters here for a very concrete reason: the hemi math *assumes* a unit normal. `remap(normal.y, -1., 1., 0., 1.)` only covers the full sky→ground range if `normal.y` really does reach `+1` (facing up) and `-1` (facing down). An unnormalized interpolated normal is shorter, so its `y` is scaled down and the gradient gets stuck in the middle — soft, gray-brown mush instead of a clear sky/ground split. Renormalize first, then the range assumptions hold.

### Seeing the normal instead of the lighting

The demo trick the README suggests: momentarily replace the color with the normal itself —

```glsl
// color = normal;
```

— and the model turns into a living color↔direction lookup table in world space, readable with the camera: the front of the monkey shades blue (normal ≈ `(0, 0, 1)`), the top green (`(0, 1, 0)`), and the surfaces facing along the red axis `(1, 0, 0)` reddish. This is a great habit when debugging any shader that consumes varyings: draw the input, verify it looks right, then draw the output.

### Isolating contributions

```glsl
lighting = ambient * 0. + hemi;
```

The fragment ends with `ambient * 0. + hemi` — multiplying ambient by zero just to watch hemi's contribution alone (a small, explicit toggle instead of commenting lines). Turning one term off at a time is how you should *see* what each lighting model contributes, exactly like hiding the engine's lights in the GUI from the earlier three.js lessons.

## Code

in repo — `apps/21_24_ambient_hemi_lights`

**TS side** — the `ShaderMaterial` is the interesting piece; everything else (monkey + cube map loading, hidden GUI folders, orbit controls, shadow-map pre-config) is starter plumbing:

```ts
// EXPLAIN: both shaders are compiled from text into a THREE.ShaderMaterial
import vertShader from './shaders/shader.vert';
import fragShader from './shaders/shader.frag';

const modelMaterial = new THREE.ShaderMaterial({
  vertexShader: vertShader,
  fragmentShader: fragShader,
});

// assign the shader material to every mesh of the Blender model
monkeyModel.scene.traverse((child) => {
  if (child.isMesh) {
    console.log(child.material.side); // 2 = THREE.DoubleSide — check the export
    child.material = modelMaterial;
    child.castShadow = true; // shadows get used in a later lesson
  }
});
```

**Vertex shader** — forward the uv, and pre-compute the world-space normal once per vertex:

```glsl
varying vec2 vUvs;
varying vec3 vNormal;   // normal, transformed into world space

void main() {
  vec4 localPosition = vec4(position, 1.);
  gl_Position = projectionMatrix * modelViewMatrix * localPosition;

  vUvs = uv;
  vNormal = (modelMatrix * vec4(normal, 0.)).xyz;  // w=0 → rotation only, no translation
}
```

**Fragment shader** — ambient + hemi, the whole lighting model in ~15 lines:

```glsl
varying vec2 vUvs;
varying vec3 vNormal;

float inverseLerp(float v, float minVal, float maxVal) {
  return (v - minVal) / (maxVal - minVal);
}
float remap(float v, float inMin, float inMax, float outMin, float outMax) {
  float t = inverseLerp(v, inMin, inMax);
  return mix(outMin, outMax, t);
}

void main() {
  vec3 baseColor = vec3(.5);   // uniform gray paint
  vec3 lighting = vec3(0.);

  // varyings arrive interpolated → restore unit length here
  vec3 normal = normalize(vNormal);

  // ---- ambient: directionless, uniform ----
  vec3 ambient = vec3(.5);

  // ---- hemi: sky above / ground below ----
  vec3 skyColor    = vec3(0., .3, .6);
  vec3 groundColor = vec3(.6, .3, .1);
  float hemiFraction = remap(normal.y, -1., 1., 0., 1.);
  vec3 hemi = mix(groundColor, skyColor, hemiFraction);

  // lighting = ambient;                      // plain ambient
  lighting = ambient * 0. + hemi;             // hemi alone, ambient zeroed

  vec3 color = baseColor * lighting;          // Hadamard product
  // color = normal;                          // visualize the normal itself

  gl_FragColor = vec4(color, 1.);
}
```

## Gotchas

- **The scene's lights are still there, just hidden.** `AmbientLight` and `DirectionalLight` are `visible = false` because lighting is being done in the shader. In the GUI, flipping one of them on lights the *standard* materials (e.g. the floor) while the shader-lit monkey ignores it — two different lighting systems disagreeing in one scene. On any real project, pick one path: engine lights *or* shader lighting, not both.
- **`scene.environment` vs `scene.background`.** The skys cube map is loaded but only `background` is set — it's a painted skybox. Setting `environment` instead would make the cube map act as a lighting source on materials, which changes how surfaces are lit and would fight the hand-written shader lighting. They're different properties with different jobs; don't swap them casually.
- **Forget `normalize(vNormal)` and hemi silently gets muddy.** The interpolated normal is shorter than 1 in the middle of faces (see the length formula in Concept), so `normal.y` never reaches `±1`, `remap` never reaches its endpoints, and the sky/ground contrast collapses. Nothing *errors* — it just looks flat and brown. This is the README's interpolation question made visible.
- **`w = 0.0` in `vec4(normal, 0.)` is load-bearing.** With `w = 1.0` the normal would be treated as a *point* and dragged along by the model's translation — a direction smuggled through the matrix as if it had a location. The `.xyz` also silently drops `w`; keep the write as a `vec4` so the matrix multiplication is well-defined.
- **Renormalization only fixes length, not flipping.** If the Blender export had double faces, inward-facing normals would light surfaces with the wrong hemisphere color. The app checks for this — `child.material.side` logging `2 = THREE.DoubleSide` — because a doubled face means a vertex has normals pointing both ways. (A non-uniform `modelMatrix` would also require the inverse-transpose for true correctness; for a rotation/scale/translation chain, normalize in the fragment covers the length.)
- **`baseColor * lighting` is a filter, not a sum.** The multiply is component-wise, so lighting can never brighten a channel past `baseColor`, and any `0` channel of light blacks out that channel of paint. When something renders unexpectedly dark, check you're not accidentally multiplying by a near-zero illumination — e.g. `vec3(0.)` lighting multiplied by the paint stays black no matter how bright you think the light is.
- **`vec3(.5)` splat.** A single scalar argument fills *every* component — `vec3(.5)` is `(0.5, 0.5, 0.5)` gray, not "0.5 in the first channel". Recall the constructor splat from [[0.23_swizzling_and_vector_operations]] before reading any of these colors as one-channel values.
- **WebGL, not WebGPU.** `main.ts` runs `WebGLRenderer` + `ShaderMaterial`/`WebGLProgram`; the `WebGPURenderer`/`renderer.init()` path is commented out of this template, same as the rest of the series (see Outdated).

## Revisit

- **Prove the interpolation dip to yourself.** Amp the effect by putting two unit vectors through `mix` in a tiny shader and plotting `length(mix(u, v, t))` across the face — it dips below 1 exactly in the middle, deeper the more the two normals differ. Then delete the `normalize` here and compare the monkey's shading. The formula in Concept is the "why"; seeing it is worth the two minutes.
- **The space question is the series' theme.** The README says it outright: vertices get transformed through several spaces, and *you* must pick which space your lighting lives in. This lesson transforms the normal to world space but leaves the *position* unforwarded. A proper diffuse light needs `dot(normal, lightDirection)` — which needs a world-space **position** `varying` too. That's the natural next step (and the engine's `DirectionalLight` math, minus the shadow map).
- **Compare with the engine's own hemisphere light.** [[12.3_hemisphere-light]] implements the same sky/ground idea as a `HemisphereLight` object with `color` + `groundColor`. Run both apps side by side and name what the shader version does that the engine version doesn't (answer: `remap`'d by your own formula, per-pixel, and fuseable with any other term you write) and vice versa (the engine version lights *standard* materials automatically, works with shadows, zero GLSL).
- **Re-enable `scene.environment` later.** Once raycasting/GLSL env-map sampling lessons come around, set `scene.environment = environmentMapTextureSkys` and watch standard materials pick up skybox lighting — then decide whether your shader wants to sample it too.
- **`castShadow`/`receiveShadow` are pre-wired for the next lessons.** The directional light has `castShadow = true`, a `1024²` shadow map and shadow-camera helpers, and the floor (`receiveShadow = true`, invisible) is waiting. When the series reaches shaders + shadows, this is the starter.
- **WGSL translation.** The adjacency of `uniform`/`varying` maps onto WGSL's `@location`-tagged pipeline uniforms, and `mix`/`remap`/`normalize` exist 1:1 — so this exact lighting model survives a WebGPU port verbatim at the math level.

## Outdated

**approach is valid** — ambient + hemi lighting hand-written in a GLSL fragment shader is a current, standard technique: it's exactly the modeling that engine lights *automate*, done explicitly. The GLSL built-ins used (`mix`, `normalize`, per-vertex `normal`) are stable in GLSL ES 3.0 and identical in WGSL.

⚡ **WebGPU note** — this lesson is *fully* doable with `WebGPURenderer` instead of `WebGLRenderer`: the varyings become `@location(0)` vertex outputs, `gl_FragColor` becomes the return value of an `@fragment` function, and `mix`/`normalize`/the normal attribute all carry across to WGSL — or, more idiomatically with three.js on WebGPU, a [TSL fragment](https://threejs.org/tsl/) would replace the raw `ShaderMaterial`. Only the plumbing changes; the lighting math is untouched. 🚀

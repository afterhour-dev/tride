# Recap

In general there are a few common ways to interpolate vectors to get that smooth transition from 1 orientation to another.

LERP is, of course, the most straightforward and performant option out there. Unfortunately the advantages stop right there.

It doesn't maintain a constant angular velocity, nor does it maintain its unit vector property since the magnitude will almost certainly change after being interpolated.

So, in short, there's very few valid reasons to use LERP directly. It's really not something that you want to use for interpolating vectors since it doesn't account for the spherical nature of rotations in the slightest.

NLURP or normalized LERP is kind of the happy medium between your basic LERP and the more complex but correct SLRP function. It is extremely performant, being only slightly more computationally costly than the basic LERP function.

You really only have to pay for the renormalize, which is really cheap in terms of CPU. There are actually hardware instructions that compute the reciprocal square root for only a few cycles, so the overall normalized call shouldn't be much. It also avoids the magnitude change problems by renormalizing the vector.

There are also downsides, of course. As we have seen, it is possible, although unlikely, to end up with a 0 vector, and it doesn't maintain constant angular velocity, which could make the transitions between 2 vectors appear to be non uniform.

But overall it's a great function that trades off a bit of accuracy for most of the performance of basic LERP.

It strikes a nice balance between the 2 and in all honesty it's probably going to be just fine for most situations. Especially when the differences in rotation or orientation of the 2 vectors isn't significant.

But when that isn't good enough though, you have another option, which is SLURP or spherical linear interpolation. The advantage of SLURP is that the interpolation of a rotation or orientation acts exactly how you'd expect it to act.

It takes the shortest path possible over the surface of the sphere, while maintaining constant angular velocity. In short, if you want to interpolate a vector this is the most accurate way to do it. The downside is that it's quite costly, quite a bit more costly than NLURP or LERP. 

But in reality if you are doing it a few times per frame that's nothing and you don't have to worry. This is more of a concern when you're performing this many, many, many times each frame, but until then you're probably okay.

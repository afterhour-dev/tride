# Rotation axis and an angle

So what quaternions give you is the ability to rotate an object via:
- rotation axis
and
- an angle. 

Or in rotor terms they interpret it as a rotation happening within the bivector specified by the vector part of the quaternion.

Like I said, the math is the same, explanations are a bit different. For the purposes of this section we will stick with the original explanation. 

![[2026-10-02_20-14.png]]

So while with Euler angles:
- we kind of rotate around each axis individually
And with matrices 
- you have to specify the linear transform, in the form of basis vectors.
![[2026-10-02_20-15.png]]

Quaternions allow you to specify:
- an axis that you are going to rotate around and an angle of how much you want to rotate. 
![[2026-10-02_20-16.png]]

In that way it's not dissimilar to when we rotated around an axis using Euler angles or rotation matrices, except that this allows us to create an arbitrary axis and rotate around that instead.

# Axis Angle

So to start, let's start with that very idea, the idea of having:
-  **axis**
and 
-  **angle**. 

And this is a way of representing rotations called `Axis Angle`, that consists of:
- unit vector `n with a hat` specifying the rotation axis
- `θ` (theta)  angle

![[2026-10-02_20-30.png]]![[2026-10-02_20-30_1.png]]

And you can use the axis angle representation to do things like transforming vectors in much the same way that you would transform a vector with a matrix.

# Rodrigues's rotation formula

So, to transform a vector by an axis angle rotation, there is something called 
- `Rodrigues's rotation formula`.

![[2026-10-02_20-32.png]]

```
v_rot = v cos θ + (k × v) sin θ + k (k · v)(1 − cos θ)
```
From what I understand, and what I found out is that k is the unit vector along the axis of rotation; **v** is the vector you want to rotate (a point’s position, relative to the origin); I also see dot product and also cross product between them 

It is also possible, but difficult to compose multiple rotations together.

Formula exists, but it starts to become a bit of an obscure topic and we are not going to bother digging into it.

# Constructing a Quaternion from Axis Angle representation

Let's start by actually constructing a quaternion because it's surprisingly easy. You can do it straight from the axis angle representation.
So if you have:
- axis `n`, and `n hat` is a unit vector, (remeber when we talked about notmalized vectors; well, we put a hat on them when representing them)
- an angle `θ` (theta), 

![[2026-10-02_23-11_1.png]]
![[2026-10-02_23-12.png]]

Then the quaternion form is:
- `cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2)(n hat)`

![[axisangleequationstuff.excalidraw|700]]
 
 Or in vector terms: 
 - `sin(θ/2)(n hat)` is vector
 And then the scalar part is
 - `cos(θ/2)`

# How to normalize 

And much like unit vectors, unit quaternions are normalized in the same way. 

![[2026-10-02_23-21.png]]

Remember! For a 3D `vector` you'd calculate the magnitude as the square root of the sum of the components squared, and then to normalize you divide the vector by that magnitude and you've got a unit vector.
![[2026-10-02_23-22.png]]

Quaternion is the same deal, the magnitude is the square root of the sum of the components, but in the case of quaternions they have four components.
So that's square root of `x` squared, plus `y` squared, plus `z` squared, plus `w` squared. And then you divide your quaternion `q` by the magnitude to get your `unit quaternion`.
![[2026-10-02_23-23.png]]

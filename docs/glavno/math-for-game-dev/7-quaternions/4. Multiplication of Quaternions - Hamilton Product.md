
# Hamilton Product

Now let's talk about quaternions by talking about multiplication, because this is the operator that lets you compose quaternion rotations in much the same way that matrix multiplication allows you to compose several linear transformations together.

![[2026-10-03_00-04.png]]

So if you have `q1` and `q2`, both of those being `unit quaternions`. 
The result `R` is equal to `q1` times `q2` and it's the result of applying `q2` and then applying `q1`, similar to what you learned for matrices.
![[2026-10-03_00-05.png]]

 The multiplication operator is, again, a little complex.

![[2026-10-04_15-31.png]]
 
 So if you represent the quaternions like this

  ![[2026-10-04_15-31_1.png]]
  So `a` plus `bi` plus `cj` plus `dk`
  
And that's multiplied by the other quaternion:
![[2026-10-03_00-14.png]]

Well let's simplify that for a second.
So we'll just do a subset of the components.
![[2026-10-03_00-11.png]]
So `a1`  plus `b1i`  times `a2`  plus `b2i`.

And when you expand that out, you get:

![[subsetmultiqater.excalidraw|700]]
That was  the **distributive law**, which many of us would have been exposed to in high school.

So when you do it with four terms on each side instead of two, so you've got `a1` plus `b1i` plus `c1j` plus `d1k` times the other quaternion

![[2026-10-03_00-18.png]]
 that ends up getting expanded out. Then you use those mysterious quaternion rules, the `i squared` equals `j squared` equals `k squared` ....
   $$
  i^{2} = j^{2} = k^{2} = ijk = -1
  $$

You use those multiplication rules that were given earlier and you use those to simplify the resulting formula. And eventually this condenses down to the following.

![[2026-10-03_00-20.png]]

```c
q1q2 = a1a2 − v1·v2 + a1v2 + a2v1 + v1 × v2
```

So the scalar portion is equal to `a1` times `a2` minus the `dot product` (also known as scalar product) between the vector portions of each of the two quaternions.

![[multiplyquaternionscalarportion.excalidraw|700]]
```c
a1a2 − v1·v2
```

And the vector portion is equal to `a1` times `v2`, the vector portion from the second quaternion, plus `a2` times `v1`, plus the `cross product`(also known as vector product) between the two vector portions, `v1` and `v2`.

![[multiplyquaternionandcrossprod.excalidraw|700]]
```c
a1v2 + a2v1 + v1 × v2
```

And yeah, that's how you multiply two quaternions together.

# Identity Quaternions

So let's get a few other things out of the way before we move on. There is the identity quaternion, which like the identity matrix amounts to a quaternion that doesn't change any quaternion it's multiplied with.

![[2026-10-03_16-18.png]]

So that's `1` plus `0i` plus `0j` plus `0k` or in vector terms `(0, 0, 0, 1)`.

![[2026-10-03_16-20.png]]

# Inverese of a Quaternion

This kind of leads to another subject, calculating the inverse of a quaternion.

![[2026-10-03_16-59.png]]

So given `q` times `q inverse`, the end result of multiplying a quaternion by its inverse is the `identity quaternion`.

![[2026-10-03_17-01.png]]

So given `q`, we need a way of, in essence, rotating backwards to nullify the first rotation.

![[2026-10-03_17-01_1.png]]

Since the `xyz` components are related to the axis of rotation, but scaled by the sine of half of the angle, then it would make sense that by simply reversing that axis of rotation we'd get a rotation going exactly the other way. I mean it makes sense in my head.

![[2026-10-03_17-03.png]]
The angle of rotation doesn't even need to change, only the sine of the axis needs to change.

So then intuitively we describe the inverse of a quaternion rotation to be `a` minus `bi` minus `cj` minus `dk`.

![[2026-10-03_17-05.png]]

Or in the vector term you basically just negate the vector portion but leave the scalar value.
![[2026-10-03_17-05_1.png]]

So that's all fine and dandy for making stuff up on the spot, it just kind of makes sense intuitively, but does it actually pan out in real life?

And it turns out it does.

That's exactly what the inverse of a unit quaternion is. It's simply negating the components of the original quaternion.

So let's say that we have some quaternion `v` and `w` where `v` has the `xyz` components.

![[2026-10-03_17-27.png]]
![[2026-10-03_17-27_1.png]]
So we'd multiply that by the `inverse negative V` and `W`.

So quaternion multiplication looks like this. The result is `w1` times `v2` plus `w2` times `v1` plus the `cross product` of `v1` and `v2`.

![[2026-10-03_17-29.png]]

And then the scalar portion is `w1` times `w2` minus the `dot product` of `v1` and `v2`.

![[2026-10-03_17-31.png]]

And we're hoping that that turns into `(0,0,0,1)`.

And so what we get is `w1` times `-v1` plus `w1` times `v1` plus the `cross product` of `v1` and `-v1`.
So you can see that these first two terms, they just kind of cancel each other out, and we're left with the `cross product` over here, but it's the `cross product` of `v` with the negative of itself, which is just `0`. So the `v` portion of the quaternion has been completely zeroed out,

So that turns into plus the `dot product` of itself. And so that turns into `cos(θ/2) squared` plus `sin(θ/2) squared`, and that's equal to 1.

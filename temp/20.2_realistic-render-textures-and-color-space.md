# 20.2 Realistic Render — Textures & Color Space

## Concept

The realistic-render chapter so far: `20.0` gave us **tone mapping + antialiasing**, `20.1` added **directional light + shadows**. This lesson makes the scene actually look *materially* real by putting **textures** on the floor and back wall (previously naked, shadow-receiving planes) and dealing with **color space**, which is what makes those textures look washed-out/whitish until you fix it.

Same cast as before: the Flight Helmet and the Blender-made gaming console (Draco-compressed glTF), the `abandoned_garage` HDR environment map, `ReinhardToneMapping` with exposure `3`, and a lil-gui panel. New additions: two `PlaneGeometry` meshes (floor + wall) sitting in a group, and a `TextureLoader` pipeline to bring in real PBR texture sets.

### The 3-texture PBR setup

Textures come from [Poly Haven](https://polyhaven.com/textures) (a favorite free source of CC0 PBR texture sets). For each material you download **three** files, not more — a well-established convention, often called the **"3-texture PBR setup"**:

1. **Diffuse / Albedo** — the base color. Goes to `material.map`.
2. **Normal (GL)** — the surface bump detail that makes light respond to scratches, mortar lines, wood grain. Goes to `material.normalMap`.
3. **ARM** (AO + Roughness + Metalness packed into one texture) — everything else the lighting needs. The same file goes to `material.aoMap`, `material.roughnessMap`, and `material.metalnessMap`:

```ts
floorMaterial.map = woodFloorAlbedoTexture;
floorMaterial.normalMap = woodFloorNormalTexture;
floorMaterial.aoMap = woodFloorARMTexture;
floorMaterial.roughnessMap = woodFloorARMTexture;
floorMaterial.metalnessMap = woodFloorARMTexture;
```

**Why this trio and not six separate textures** (diffuse, AO, roughness, metalness, normal, displacement)?

- **AO** already lives in the ARM texture's **red** channel — loading it standalone is redundant.
- **Roughness** is the **green** channel of ARM — redundant again.
- **Displacement** is *skipped on purpose*: it either requires real geometry tessellation (subdividing the mesh a lot) or parallax occlusion (a shader trick) — both add real complexity and cost. For real-time work, the normal map already fakes enough surface detail for the eye. (If you do want it later for close-ups: `displacementMap` + `displacementScale`, on a mesh with enough subdivided geometry to actually deform.)
- Net: **3 texture fetches instead of 6**, for only a minor loss in surface realism. This is a performance-conscious convention — matches this chapter's whole theme of buying realism cheaply.

**For a brick wall / wood floor specifically** this is a great fit: brick and wood are non-metals, so ARM's **blue** (metalness) channel is ~0 anyway — no loss. Displacement *would* help mortar lines or wood grain read as more 3D under grazing light, but it's not worth the cost unless you're doing close-up hero shots.

### Why PNG for the normal map (not JPG)

Format choice for the download: **`png` for the normal map, `jpg` for the other two.** Normal maps encode a *direction per pixel* (the surface normal, roughly `RGB → XYZ`). Direction data must survive **losslessly** — JPG's block artifacts produce tiny but real discontinuities in the encoded normals, which show up as flicker/wrong lighting on the surface, especially in motion. Base color and ARM can tolerate lossy compression fine (small high-frequency errors in albedo are invisible; tiny errors in AO/roughness/metal are masked by the lighting math). Keep `png` on the normal only.

### Normal (GL) vs Normal (DX) — which green channel to pick

Three.js expects the **GL (OpenGL) convention**: the green channel encodes the Y-axis of the normal map with **Y+ up**. The **DX (DirectX)** variant is the same texture vertically flipped (used by Unity and Unreal). If you download a set that offers both, pick **Normal (GL)** — a DX texture loaded with GL semantics flips all the bumps vertically (a hole becomes a ridge). Blender and three.js agree on GL.

### The generic `TextureLoader` / `LoadingManager` pipeline

Textures get their own loader, mirroring the HDR loader from `19.x` and the model loader from `16.x`:

```ts
const textureLoadingManager = new THREE.LoadingManager();
const textureLoader = new THREE.TextureLoader(textureLoadingManager);

const woodFloorAlbedoTexture = await textureLoader.loadAsync('/textures/.../diff_1k.jpg');
```

`LoadingManager` callbacks (`onProgress`, `onLoad`, `onError`, `onStart`) give you load feedback per pipeline. The same pattern appears three times in this app (HDRI, textures, models) — one manager + loader pair per asset category.

### Color space — why the textures look whitish

The moment you assign the textures, the floor and wall look **washed out / whitish**. The culprit is **color space**, and it only bites the *visible* textures (the albedo) — exactly the ones the eye judges.

The story:

- Color is stored in files **gamma-encoded** to match the human eye's sensitivity (log-ish, not linear — the eye is far more sensitive to dark-to-mid tones than bright ones). For web/photo textures that's **sRGB** gamma ≈ 2.2.
- But all the renderer's **lighting math runs in linear light** (double it, double the light — that only works in a linear space). So textures must be *decoded* from sRGB into the linear working space before materials use them.
- A texture's `colorSpace` tells the renderer what encoding the file uses, so it can decode it correctly:

```ts
woodFloorAlbedoTexture.colorSpace = THREE.SRGBColorSpace;
crackedConcretAlbedoTexture.colorSpace = THREE.SRGBColorSpace;
```

The **default is `THREE.NoColorSpace`** — color management *off*, the sampler hands the bytes to the lighting math as-is (treated as linear). sRGB-encoded pixels treated as linear come out **too bright and desaturated** — your whitish floor and wall. Annotate albedo textures with either `SRGBColorSpace` or `LinearSRGBColorSpace` (the latter when the file is already linear, e.g. some HDR/scientific sources) — choose one, since a texture decoded with the wrong one will be wrongly bright or wrongly dark.

### Why the models don't need a color-space change

The Flight Helmet and the Blender-made console are loaded via `GLTFLoader` and look right with no touch. Why? **The glTF file already carries the texture's color-space info**: the [glTF spec's KTX2 texture extension](https://docs.polyhaven.com/gltf/color-space/) lets exporters declare sRGB vs linear per-texture, and three.js (r165+) honors it on load. That answers the README's open question — *"is this true, and does my Blender model have it?"*: yes, Blender writes this on export by default (its glTF exporter tags base-color textures as sRGB unless you deliberately configure linear workflows). Nothing to fix on either model.

The environment map sits outside all this: it's already loaded as HDR (`2k.hdr`) with linear data, so it never runs through the sRGB decode path.

### Grouping the floor & wall with the models

Both planes go into a `floorAndWallGroup` (rotated `y = PI/4` to face the camera), and that group plus both models go into an `allGroup` — because this app is about *seeing* things, the whole layout can be rotated live from the GUI to poke at the shadow problem from any angle. One detail to remember: the **wall needs `receiveShadow = true`** too, exactly like the floor and models — a receiving surface that forgets its flag simply never shows shadows.

### The wall-shadow mystery (the README's whole saga)

The centerpiece of this lesson is a genuine unsolved trap — worth reading carefully:

> Shadows work on the **floor** but not on the **wall** — *whenever a normal map is set on the wall's material*. With no textures, or textures minus the normal map, wall shadows appear. The same material config on the floor works. Rotating the wall horizontal makes its shadows reappear. Moving the light also produces a weird "sweet spot": in some positions the wall suddenly gets a sharp highlight and shadows work; elsewhere they vanish.

Things tried and ruled out: shadow map 512→1024, light direction, `shadow.camera.near/far`, `bias` and `normalBias` (both brutally tweaked in the GUI), environment map differences. The only thing that worked:

```ts
wallMaterial.normalScale = new THREE.Vector2(0.2, 0.2);
wallMaterial.needsUpdate = true;
```

The honest state of affairs: **this is empirically fixed by *damping the normal map* (`normalScale` scales the normal map's effect), not truly root-caused.** But the pieces point somewhere coherent:

- The shadow pass is a *depth comparison* rendered from the light's perspective at finite resolution (512×512 here). The material shader then does its **own** per-pixel shadow lookup, and `normalBias` displaces the sample point *along the surface normal* by a small world-space amount to avoid acne.
- A **strong normal map perturbs the surface normal a lot** — at the wall's geometry (vertical, under grazing light from a light placed high at `(5, 6, 5)`, further skewed by the group rotation), even a fraction of that tiny displacement can push the shadow lookup across shadow-map texels and onto the *lit* side → shadows read as "always lit", or degenerate into acne.
- The moving-light "sweet spot" fits this too: as the light's *angle* to the wall changes, the grazing incidence and the normal perturbation interact strongly — some angles tip the comparison one way, some the other.
- The env map is **not** implicated — and the README is right to suspect it isn't: an environment map has no geometry, so it **cannot cast shadows at all** (`19.x` only gives lighting/reflections). It's purely the light + shadow map + normal map interaction.

So: damping the normal map (`normalScale ≈ 0.2`) or removing it is the practical lever; the *why* — the exact interplay of `normalBias`, shadow-map texel size, and grazing angle on a normal-mapped vertical plane — is a genuinely good deeper dig (see Revisit).

## Code

in repo - `apps/20_2_realistic_render_textures_and_color_space`

```ts
import * as THREE from 'three/webgpu';
import { TextureLoader } from 'three/addons/loaders/TextureLoader.js'; // or via loading manager

// one loader pipeline per asset category
const textureLoadingManager = new THREE.LoadingManager();
const textureLoader = new THREE.TextureLoader(textureLoadingManager);

// 3-texture PBR set per material
const woodFloorAlbedoTexture = await textureLoader.loadAsync('/textures/old_wooden_floor_03_1k/old_wooden_floor_03_diff_1k.jpg');
const woodFloorNormalTexture  = await textureLoader.loadAsync('/textures/old_wooden_floor_03_1k/old_wooden_floor_03_nor_gl_1k.png');
const woodFloorARMTexture     = await textureLoader.loadAsync('/textures/old_wooden_floor_03_1k/old_wooden_floor_03_arm_1k.jpg');

// albedo textures are gamma (sRGB) encoded — decode them or they look whitish
woodFloorAlbedoTexture.colorSpace = THREE.SRGBColorSpace;

// assign the trio; ARM feeds three channels at once
const floorMaterial = new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial();
floorMaterial.map = woodFloorAlbedoTexture;
floorMaterial.normalMap = woodFloorNormalTexture;
floorMaterial.aoMap = woodFloorARMTexture;
floorMaterial.roughnessMap = woodFloorARMTexture;
floorMaterial.metalnessMap = woodFloorARMTexture;
// floorMaterial.roughness = 1;  // multipliers on maps: leave at 1!
// floorMaterial.metalness = 1;

const floorMesh = new THREE.Mesh(new THREE.PlaneGeometry(8, 8), floorMaterial);
floorMesh.receiveShadow = true; // don't forget — receiving is per-mesh
```

## Gotchas

- **`metalness` / `roughness` scale *multiply* the maps** — they act as multipliers on the texture values, not replacements. Leave them at `1` when you supply `metalnessMap`/`roughnessMap`, otherwise you're dimming the texture's own signal. (`// EXPLAIN:` flagged in the code.)
- **Normal maps must be lossless — PNG, never JPG.** JPG block artifacts corrupt the encoded surface normals → wrong/flickering lighting.
- **Pick Normal (GL), not Normal (DX).** DX flips the green channel's Y direction; a DX map read as GL turns ridges into grooves. Unity/Unreal want DX; three.js, Blender, OpenGL want GL.
- **The whitish-texture moment is a color-space symptom, not a lighting one.** Default `colorSpace` is `NoColorSpace` (color management off). Annotate visible albedo textures with `THREE.SRGBColorSpace` (or `LinearSRGBColorSpace` if the file is already linear). NoColorSpace + sRGB data = too bright/desaturated.
- **Models from glTF don't need this** — the file carries the color space. But *hand-loaded* textures (yours, from Poly Haven) come with no metadata — that's why you set it yourself. Don't "fix" what the loader already knows.
- **A strong normal map can kill shadows on surfaces at grazing angles** (this lesson's mystery). `normalScale` (`THREE.Vector2`) damps the normal map's effect — `(0.2, 0.2)` resurrected the wall's shadows. Investigate before assuming shadow-camera settings are wrong.
- **Material changes after construction need `needsUpdate = true`** — the material re-uploads its data only when flagged (the GUI's normal-map toggle sets it explicitly).
- **The receiving flag is per-mesh and easy to forget**: `wallMesh.receiveShadow = true`, `floorMesh.receiveShadow = true` — the wall in particular, since "behind" planes get mentally skipped.
- `shadow.radius` (blur) **does nothing with `PCFSoftShadowMap`** — recap of `20.1`; only plain `PCFShadowMap` blurs.

## Revisit

- **Root-cause the wall-shadow mystery properly.** The mechanism sketched in Concept (normal map perturbation × `normalBias` × shadow-map texel size at grazing incidence) is plausible but not proven. A solid future session: toggle `basic`/`PCF`/`VSM` shadow algorithms on the wall, raise `mapSize` to 2048, sweep `normalBias` in tiny steps *with* and *without* the normal map — and screenshot the shadow map itself at each step. That will tell you whether the loss is in the depth pass (map generation) or the per-pixel lookup (material shader). Probably keep `normalScale = 0.2` as a documented workaround either way.
- **Is 1K texture resolution enough?** For mid shots behind two models — yes, comfortably. Rule of thumb: keep the on-screen texel-to-pixel ratio near 1–2; 1K covers a floor/wall unless you orbit close. Re-check the wood floor at high zoom in the GUI before deciding to grab 2K.
- **Verify the Blender color-space claim.** "Blender exports sRGB tags by default" — confirmed by the model looking right here, but it's worth one explicit check: open `console-for-export.gltf` and confirm the base-color textures carry the sRGB annotation.
- **GL vs DX panic-avoidance.** If a downloaded set's normals look inverted, first check the variants offered — you likely grabbed the DX one. Getting burned once makes it stick.
- **Displacement, when you're ready** — close-up hero shots of the brick wall would benefit: `displacementMap` + `displacementScale` on a tessellated mesh. Real cost, real payoff, skip for now.

## Outdated

approach is valid

> 💡 **This is a WebGPU-first lesson.** The renderer is `THREE.WebGPURenderer` from `three/webgpu` — but everything here (texture maps on `MeshStandardMaterial`, `colorSpace`, `normalScale`) is identical on the classic WebGL renderer, as of the r165+ material/color-management rework. One WebGL note: older tutorials predating r165 won't have `colorSpace` at all (colors were just blindly piped) — if you see whitish textures in old WebGL code, that lesson predates color management and the fix is the same `SRGBColorSpace` annotation.
